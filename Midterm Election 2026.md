## How to Read This Document

Some terms of art appear throughout. Each is explained here once, in plain language, and used in that sense from then on.

**Midterm election.** In the American system, the president serves four years, but every member of the House of Representatives and roughly one-third of the Senate face the voters every two years. The election held midway through a presidential term is the *midterm*. On November 3, 2026, all 435 House seats, 35 Senate seats (33 regular plus two special elections), 36 governorships, roughly 6,100 state legislative seats, 145 statewide ballot measures, and thousands of local offices are on the ballot.

**PVI.** The Partisan Voting Index measures how a state or district leans politically compared to the country as a whole. "R+7" means the area voted about seven percentage points more Republican than the nation in recent elections. PVI is a thermometer, not a destiny.

**Dark money.** Political spending by nonprofit organizations that are not required to disclose their donors. Imagine a sealed envelope inside a sealed box: the box (the super PAC) must report what it spends, but the envelope inside it — the nonprofit that supplied the money — never has to say whose check it was.

**Super PAC.** A political committee that may raise and spend unlimited sums to advocate for or against candidates, but may not donate directly to them or coordinate with their campaigns — at least, not until a 2026 Supreme Court decision loosened the coordination rules, which this document covers in its proper place.

**501(c)(4).** A type of nonprofit — "social welfare" organizations — that can spend heavily in politics without revealing donors. These are the sealed envelopes.

**Conduit / earmarked giving.** When a donor gives to a committee (like AIPAC's PAC) with instructions to pass it to a specific candidate, the recipient sees only the committee's name. The money arrives with the donor's fingerprints washed off.

**Ranked-choice voting (RCV).** Voters rank candidates in order of preference. If nobody wins a majority of first choices, the last-place candidate is eliminated and their voters' second choices are counted instead, repeating until someone has a majority. Maine uses this system for federal races; Alaska uses a variant. In 2026, Alaska is voting on whether to repeal it.

**Certification.** The formal, legally binding declaration of results by state authorities — distinct from election-night projections, which are news organizations' estimates. Only certified results confer office.

**Recount.** A second tabulation ordered when margins fall below state-defined triggers (automatic) or requested by candidates (petition). All fifty states differ; the document's research files carry the complete table.

**Contested election.** The constitutional procedure by which Congress itself judges its own members' elections — the backstop if a state count is disputed, last invoked at scale in the 1870s.

**Naked ballot.** A mail ballot returned without its required secrecy envelope. In Pennsylvania — which offers no statewide cure process — a naked ballot is discarded.

**Discharge petition.** A device by which a majority of the House forces a bill to a floor vote over leadership's objection. One such petition, forcing a congressional stock-trading ban toward a vote, became a campaign issue when signatories included members whose own trades were under scrutiny.

**Cloture.** The Senate's sixty-vote threshold to end debate on most legislation — the arithmetic behind every "49–50" vote in this document, since failing cloture means a bill never reaches final passage.

**Trifecta.** One party holding the governorship and both legislative chambers of a state — the prize in the state-legislative money war, because trifectas draw the maps.

**Revolving door.** The movement of officials and staff between government and the industries they regulate. The document's research files count 13,519 lobbyists with prior government service and 460 former members currently lobbying.

**Insider trading / stock trading.** Members of Congress may legally trade individual securities — the loophole every trading-ban bill in this document addresses. A blind trust is the disclosure-avoidance device in which an official's assets are managed without their knowledge; several members pledged blind trusts and later walked the pledges back, on the record.

**Joint fundraising committee.** A vehicle through which multiple campaigns or committees raise money together and split it by formula — supercharged by the 2026 Supreme Court decision to allow effectively unlimited coordination.

**Soft money.** The pre-2002 category of unlimited party donations nominally for "party building," banned by the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act and reborn through the dark-money channels described in this document.

**AdImpact.** The industry-standard advertising-tracking firm whose spending figures appear throughout this document; its projections and actuals are the campaign world's common yardstick.

**Citizens United.** The 2010 Supreme Court decision permitting corporations and unions to spend unlimited sums on independent political expenditures — the foundation on which the super PAC and 501(c)(4) architecture of Part V rests.

**Pay-to-play.** The exchange of campaign contributions for government action — the specific crime alleged in some files in this document and denied in all of them, none adjudicated before Election Day.

**Filibuster.** The practice of extended debate by which a minority blocks Senate action — preserved only by custom, and the object of reform proposals in both parties.

**A note on sources and fairness.** This document was assembled under a strict discipline: collect, don't adopt. When the administration says $260.7 billion in fraud was uncovered, that claim appears. When its critics document thousands of conflicts of interest, those appear too. When a poll shows one thing and a model shows another, both appear. Nothing in these pages decides for the reader which ledger is true. The reader decides. What the pages guarantee is that the evidence arrived intact.

**What a research dive is.** This book was assembled from 140 research dives — 140 separate expeditions into the public record, conducted October 23–24, 2026. Here is what one dive looks like, so the number means something concrete. Each dive began with a target — a race, a network, a court case, a loose thread left open by the dive before it. It then ran two to four rounds of searching, asking the search engine four to eight questions in total: who funds this candidate, what did this investigation find, which filing carries this number. Each search returned ten to thirty sources — news reports, government filings, court documents, campaign-finance databases — and each source that mattered was read, not skimmed: names, dollar figures, dates, quotes, and critically, who said it, because a court filing carries more weight than a campaign press release, and this book labels both as what they are. Everything found was written into a dated file of dense notes — two thousand to five thousand words per dive, no summaries, every fact carrying its source tag, every correction written where it belonged, every open question flagged so no hole would be mistaken for a finding. Across 140 dives that is roughly six hundred to eleven hundred search questions, two thousand to four thousand sources read, and some four hundred thirty thousand words of raw research notes — the archive beneath this book. A human researcher doing the same work would measure it in weeks. What the number means in plain terms: no corner of this election went unlooked-at. Every race was checked. Every dollar was traced. Every name was found. Every claim was checked against its source. And every gap was labeled honestly rather than filled in.

**What is open.** This record was compiled on October 23, 2026 — eleven days before the vote. Certain facts did not yet exist: Election Day weather, final forecaster updates, last-minute donor disclosures, a Brazil runoff result, and late economic data. Each open item appears in its proper place, marked plainly as open. Nothing in this document pretends to knowledge it does not have.

## PART I — THE ROAD TO NOVEMBER 3

*What this part covers: the eleven months that made the election — the enforcement surge, the killings, the capture, the shutdowns, the war, the fraud trial, the files, and the redistricting war — told in the order they happened, so that every later chapter can be understood as the consequence of something you have already read.*

### 1. The Shape of the Fight

The 119th Congress gaveled in with a Republican House majority so thin it could vanish in a single special election, and a Republican Senate holding 53 seats against 47 held or aligned with Democrats. Two independents — Bernie Sanders of Vermont and Angus King of Maine — caucus with the Democrats, which is why the majority math is sometimes written 53–47 and sometimes 53–45–2.

The stakes were plain on their face. A net gain of four Senate seats hands Democrats the majority; a net gain of four House seats does the same. Behind those numbers sit two years of accumulated events — a war, an assassination, three citizen killings by federal agents, three government shutdowns, a billion-dollar fraud trial, the largest supply disruption in the history of the oil market, and the most expensive midterm campaign ever recorded. This document covers all of it, in the order a citizen would need it.

### 2. December 2025: The Operation Begins

On December 1, 2025, the Department of Homeland Security launched Operation Metro Surge — what it called the largest immigration enforcement operation in American history — in the Minneapolis–Saint Paul metropolitan area. The force grew from roughly 100 agents in the first week to about 4,000 by mid-January — a deployment roughly six times the size of the Minneapolis police department. The operation, billed as targeting violent offenders, swept up people in grocery stores, outside schools, and at court appearances. A Richmond School of Law review of arrest records found that only about 5 percent of detainees had convictions for violent crimes; 73 percent had no criminal conviction at all.

A warning network of whistles and horns spread through immigrant neighborhoods; church basements and coffee shops became pop-up aid stations; school attendance dropped. On December 31, 2025 — the operation's one-month mark — an off-duty ICE agent named Brian Palacios, living in the same Northridge, Los Angeles apartment complex as Keith Porter Jr., a 43-year-old father of two, shot and killed Porter after Porter fired a rifle into the air celebrating New Year's Eve. The Department of Homeland Security called it a response to an active shooter. Porter's family disputed it; a Los Angeles Times investigation later reported court filings accusing the agent of child abuse and racism, and the family sought a hate-crime investigation. No charges have been filed.

### 3. January 2026: The Crucible Month

January 2026 contained, compressed into thirty-one days, nearly everything that would define the election year.

**The first shutdown.** On January 31, half of the federal government shut down for four days after Senate Democrats withdrew support for a Department of Homeland Security funding bill in the aftermath of the Porter killing, demanding reforms and a clean extension instead. A deal passed February 3 funding DHS only through February 13 — setting the fuse for a second, longer shutdown.

**Renée Good, January 7.** Renée Nicole Macklin Good, a 37-year-old poet and mother of three, was shot three times by ICE agent Jonathan David Ross in south Minneapolis after dropping a child at school. Video showed her car moving away from the agent when he fired. The Department of Homeland Security called it self-defense and described her actions as "an act of domestic terrorism"; the Hennepin County medical examiner ruled the death a homicide. A YouGov/Statista poll found 50 percent of Americans said the shooting was not justified, 30 percent said it was. The FBI took control of the investigation and blocked state investigators from case material; six federal prosecutors in Minneapolis resigned after the Justice Department declined to open a civil-rights probe; the department's public integrity section declined to investigate. Bruce Springsteen and U2 recorded songs about her; an estimated 50,000 people marched in Minneapolis on January 23 in subzero cold, in what labor historians called perhaps the first general-strike-scale protest in the United States since 1947.

**The Maduro capture, early January.** In the same days, U.S. special forces captured Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro in Caracas and flew him to New York to face the drug-trafficking indictment against him; his vice president, Delcy Rodríguez, was sworn in as interim president. President Trump announced the United States would "run" Venezuela. The United Nations continued to list Maduro as head of state. Trump privately nominated Secretary of State Marco Rubio as the operation's quarterback; Rubio simultaneously served as acting national security adviser. Trump also threatened to annex Greenland by force, then backed down; 75 percent of Americans opposed taking Greenland. In Congress, a war-powers resolution on Venezuela was sunk procedurally after administration pressure flipped two Republican senators who had previously supported it.

**Alex Pretti, January 24.** Alex Jeffrey Pretti, a 37-year-old intensive-care nurse at the Minneapolis VA, a legal concealed-carry permit holder with no criminal record, was shot roughly ten times in under five seconds by Border Patrol agents during the same operation, after moving to help a woman an agent had shoved to the ground. Bystander video appeared to show an agent removing Pretti's handgun from his waistband moments before the fatal shots; an officer at the scene can be heard saying "I got the gun. I got the gun." The medical examiner ruled it a homicide. Within hours, White House adviser Stephen Miller publicly called Pretti a "domestic terrorist"; the Department of Homeland Security said he "wanted to do maximum damage and massacre law enforcement" — statements the administration later walked back, with Trump himself saying days later that the two Minneapolis incidents "should not have happened." The two agents were identified by ProPublica as Jesus Ochoa and Raymundo Gutierrez; both were placed on administrative leave. No one has been charged.

**Alex Castro, January 14.** Between the two Minneapolis fatalities, ICE agent Christian Castro shot and wounded Julio Cesar Sosa-Celis, a 24-year-old Venezuelan man legally present in the United States, firing through a closed front door. Castro later testified he had been attacked with a broom and shovel and had struggled with the man for about three minutes. Surveillance video showed the interaction lasted eleven seconds. In May, Hennepin County Attorney Mary Moriarty charged Castro with assault; a federal grand jury indicted him in September for materially false statements — the first criminal charge against an ICE officer of the era. Separately, federal prosecutor Matthew Evans, who had pushed for more serious civil-rights charges, was fired.

**Walz withdraws; Klobuchar rises.** On January 5, Minnesota Governor Tim Walz withdrew from the governor's race he had entered months earlier, citing the fraud scandal examined in Part V and the political climate; he said he had "zero sadness and zero regret." On January 29, Senator Amy Klobuchar announced she would leave the Senate to run for governor — meaning both of Minnesota's Senate seats would effectively be in play.

**The second shutdown begins, February 14.** DHS funding lapsed again — and this time the standoff lasted 76 days, the longest Department of Homeland Security shutdown in history. Democrats passed resolutions to fund all of DHS except immigration enforcement; the Republican House refused for weeks; about 61,000 TSA officers missed two paychecks, 510 TSA employees resigned, and security lines at major airports stretched for hours. The trigger, in the president's own framing, was the SAVE Act — the proof-of-citizenship voting bill that had passed the House but not the Senate, and that he insisted must pass as the price of reopening the department. It never did. On April 30, the House passed the Senate's version by voice vote, and the president signed it the same day. The full-year arithmetic: the White House emergency order that eventually paid the officers was signed March 27. Across the fiscal year, combined funding lapses reached 161 days — forty-four percent of the year — across three shutdowns, a cascade that began with the October 1 to November 12, 2025 full-government lapse and its eight-day E-Verify outage.

### 4. February and March: The War Begins

On February 28, 2026, the United States and Israel struck Iran, beginning a war that was more than seven months old by Election Day. Six American soldiers were killed in a Kuwait strike in the opening days. The war's congressional story is written in two votes: on September 15, the Senate failed to advance the CLARITY Act — a cryptocurrency market-structure bill — by one vote, 49 to 50, after every Democrat and four Republicans opposed it; on September 28, the Senate rejected an Iran war powers resolution, 49 to 50. The executive branch has since operated the war on its claimed authorities, without restriction. The war's economic mechanics became a campaign issue in their own right: the supply loss in March — roughly ten million barrels a day — was more than double the 1973 oil shock, the largest in history, and gasoline rose from $2.80 before the war to above $4.30. Two senior Iranian officials told Reuters their strategy was to keep prices high to influence the American vote; the president accused Iran of inventing tanker incidents to raise prices and said he would not attack before the election. A June memorandum of understanding purported to grant Iran shipping administration rights in exchange for oil-export waivers; each side accused the other of violating it. On October 11, the president announced an energy ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine tied to a diesel-for-sanctions-relief deal — six senators, including two Republicans in their own difficult re-elections, publicly demanded its reversal, calling it a financial lifeline for Putin. On October 18, Vladimir Putin proposed a three-way summit with the American and Chinese presidents to decide Ukraine's fate; the proposal sat unanswered as the vote approached. In Congress, the bipartisan response hardened: members of both parties drafted a resolution to "unequivocally reaffirm" the alliance after the election — the legislative backstop against the administration's drift, filed but not passed before the vote. The household arithmetic, per Moody's Mark Zandi: the war cost the average household roughly $2,000 — $1,100 in energy, $540 in higher interest rates, $360 in military-related costs — and the energy portion was growing by about $50 a week. Brent crude traded above $113 a barrel in late October, roughly thirty dollars of it a geopolitical premium; the Federal Reserve, under a new chairman confirmed in May, had already raised rates once in September and priced a second for December — the first tightening cycle into an election since 2022. One historical echo, from a wealth manager's ledger: only five presidents since Truman polled lower, and each of their parties lost or did not run again.

By late March, polling showed the president's approval rating reaching record lows — Reuters/Ipsos recorded 31 percent — amid the war, tariffs, and the concurrent shutdown.

### 5. The Spring: A Wave of Records

The months between March and July produced, in sequence:

**The largest fraud case in Minnesota history, closed.** On March 19, 2025, a federal jury had convicted Aimee Bock and an associate on all counts in the Feeding Our Future scheme — roughly $250 to $300 million stolen from federal child-nutrition programs, the largest pandemic-era fraud in America. On May 21, 2026, a federal judge sentenced Bock to 500 months — forty-one years and eight months — calling her the "epicenter" of "a vortex of fraud." That same day, federal officials announced $90 million in new Medicaid and welfare fraud charges and a national strike force. The estimates of the broader Minnesota fraud had escalated over the year from hundreds of millions to a federal prosecutor's statement that half or more of the $18 billion that passed through fourteen state programs since 2018 might be stolen — a claim the administration amplified and the state's political establishment contested. Ninety people were charged across the schemes; more than 80 were of Somali descent, in a state that hosts the largest Somali diaspora in America. Representative Ilhan Omar said the community should not be blamed as a whole. The fraud scandal's arithmetic, assembled across the investigations: the Feeding Our Future conviction rested on roughly $250–300 million in stolen child-nutrition funds; the housing-services program had grown from a $2.6 million annual estimate to $100 million before termination; a federal prosecutor's escalation claimed half or more of $18 billion through fourteen state programs might be stolen; the president's figure reached $19 billion, with an allegation of diversion to Somalia and into the hands of al-Shabaab — an allegation a Treasury investigation was examining. The administration's own consolidated accounting, published in September, claimed $260.7 billion in fraud uncovered across the government since January 2025, with $72 billion a year stopped. The two sets of figures rest on different methods and different definitions; both are in this record. The government-shrinkage project of the term’s first year — the Department of Government Efficiency, the Elon Musk-driven effort informally dissolved eight months before its own statutory sunset and formally closed on July 4, 2026 — claimed $215 billion in savings. Independent estimates put the implemented figure at $55 to $85 billion and the net figure, after costs and reversed actions, at $40 to $65 billion; federal spending rose over the period. Its reductions reached roughly a quarter of the Health and Human Services department, a student-aid system serving 43 million borrowers, and the agency that tracked firefighter cancers — and its most consequential reversal came quietly, when nuclear-stockpile management functions were restored after early cuts. By the campaign’s end, DOGE functioned in Democratic advertising as a one-word shorthand for cuts to hospitals, veterans’ care, and schools; the administration’s own assessment was, in Musk’s words, a little bit successful.

**A speaker assassinated.** On June 14, 2025 — before the war, before the Minneapolis killings — a former gubernatorial appointee named Vance Luther Boelter had impersonated a police officer and murdered Minnesota House Speaker Emerita Melissa Hortman and her husband Mark in their Brooklyn Park home, after wounding State Senator John Hoffman and his wife in a related attack. Boelter's writings contained a target list of Democratic officials. He surrendered after the largest manhunt in state history. He pleaded not guilty; federal charges could carry the death penalty, a decision resting with the Justice Department. A September 16, 2025 special election restored the House to a 67–67 tie.

**No Kings.** Between the Kirk assassination and the war, a protest movement assembled what organizers described as the largest single-day demonstrations in American history. More than eight million people across 3,300 events on March 28, 2026, in the fourth and largest wave — the earlier waves had drawn five million and seven million. The movement explicitly named the Minneapolis killings. And by the final pre-election wave on October 17, it had pivoted from message to turnout under the slogan "Too Big to Rig."

**Charlie Kirk.** On September 10, 2025, conservative activist Charlie Kirk was assassinated by a single rifle shot from 400 feet away while speaking at Utah Valley University. The suspect, Tyler Robinson, faces a death-penalty prosecution; a judge ruled in September 2026 that the case would proceed to trial, finding the evidence overwhelming. Kirk's widow took over his organization; the president awarded him the Presidential Medal of Freedom posthumously. His death, nine months before the vote, shadowed the entire conservative movement's campaign.

**The Epstein files.** On January 30, 2026, the Justice Department released 3.5 million pages of materials concerning Jeffrey Epstein under an act of Congress, with roughly 2.5 million pages withheld. The release produced a Commerce Secretary's admission of a 2012 lunch on Epstein's island with his wife and four children, contradicting his earlier denials; the compelled testimony of Bill and Hillary Clinton — the first former president ever compelled to testify before Congress; and a recurring political weapon: the files were cited in attack advertising by both parties. A letter from a federal judge later noted that the Attorney General's office had conceded the act's disclosure requirements had been violated, with millions of pages withheld "often on the basis of legal privilege." Ghislaine Maxwell — imprisoned on a twenty-year trafficking sentence — gave a deposition taking the Fifth on every question and, through her attorney, made the offer explicitly on the record: she would testify, including that both the current and a former president were innocent of wrongdoing, if granted clemency. The White House said no leniency was being discussed; the Attorney General said Maxwell should hopefully die in prison. The question of who ordered her transfer to a minimum-security camp was never publicly answered. In Britain, the files' fallout reached the royal family: the king's brother was arrested on his sixty-sixth birthday on suspicion of misconduct in public office for forwarding trade documents to Epstein, stripped of his titles — the first senior royal arrest since the seventeenth century — and, in October, a judge ruled the police searches of his home unlawful while the investigation continued.

**The money-for-favor stories.** In the final weeks of the campaign, four major money-for-favor stories converged: a ProPublica investigation into Senator Susan Collins and a defense contractor's $8 million Navy contract; a Washington Post investigation into Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton's legal opinions benefiting donors; a New York Times investigation into a $50 million consulting contract awarded to the president's former personal attorney weeks before a Venezuelan banker's pardon; and an Associated Press investigation into federal disaster aid flowing preferentially to Republican governors. The principals, named. The Texas file runs through **Anosh Ahmed** and **Sameer Suhail**, the hospital-fraud defendants whose $190,000 to Ken Paxton included $25,000 given days after their indictment. The Venezuela oil file runs through **Alejandro Betancourt López**, the contractor who partnered with the administration on a deal granting a century of rights over seventeen oil fields — signed by a co-investor federal prosecutors had identified as an unindicted co-conspirator in a prior embezzlement scheme — and whose own money-laundering investigations in three countries remain unresolved. Each is documented in full, with both sides' responses, in Part IX. None was resolved before Election Day. All of them were on the ballot. One watchdog's accounting names twenty members and candidates in its annual corruption list, with the receipts attached. ### 6. The Summer: The Ledger of Force

Between June and September, federal immigration operations killed two more men — Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, a 52-year-old Houston homebuilder with no criminal record, shot during a traffic operation on July 7, and Johan Sebastián Durán Guerrero, a 25-year-old Colombian father, shot in Biddeford, Maine on July 13 after his car was rammed and struck by five bullets. After the second killing, DHS ordered a nationwide pause on vehicle stops; the president overturned it within a day on social media. On September 20, an agent in Austin shot Wilber Rafael Garcés Pérez, a 28-year-old DoorDash driver with a work permit, in the back during a license-plate check; a federal judge halted his deportation, and on October 1 — the day a hearing revealed agents' routine practice of running random license plates — DHS issued new guidance banning high-speed chases.

The structural statistic beneath the individual cases: Wired reported that more than 25 people had been killed in ICE-agent shootings since 2015 without a single criminal indictment, until the Castro charge in September 2026. Fifty-six people died in ICE custody between January 2025 and August 2026 — the highest annual pace since records began in 2004.

The disaster-agency record belongs in the same season. The administration's FEMA administrator — confirmed in August 2026 after having been fired in 2025 for testifying that the agency should not be eliminated, and polygraphed by his own department in the interim — began implementing a shift of disaster response to the states in October, two weeks before the election. He told a conference that states should build financial reserves so recovery could start immediately. The agency had shed more than 4,300 employees in the prior year and held more than a thousand open disaster declarations. An Associated Press analysis found the president had approved roughly 80 percent of Republican governors' disaster requests against 60 percent of Democratic governors'. The administration called the figure a function of application quality, not politics. Twenty-five states and the District of Columbia had sued the department over election-security conditions attached to counterterrorism grants — the same department whose acting secretary spent October demanding handover of state voter data.

### 7. The Map War

While the human story unfolded, a quieter war redrawn the battlefield itself. Between July 2025 and September 2026, more than a dozen states redrew their congressional maps mid-decade — the largest coordinated mid-decade redistricting in modern history — producing a net Republican gain of roughly twelve seats and a Democratic gain of one, with thirteen competitive districts dissolved. Texas drew five new Republican-leaning seats; California answered with Proposition 50, a Democratic map funded by $100 million of mostly dark money, approved by voters 64 to 36 in November 2025. Missouri's map was struck down repeatedly and finally blocked by the Supreme Court on September 25. Virginia's voters approved a Democratic map in April 2026, only to have the state Supreme Court invalidate it. Every map in this paragraph was challenged in court; most survived. The practical effect, as one analyst put it: the 2026 wave would crash not against a natural shoreline but against a seawall built for it.

### 8. By the Numbers, Going In

By late October, the environment was measurable:

- The generic congressional ballot — the national survey question asking voters which party they plan to support — averaged a Democratic lead of eight to nine points, up from four to six in the summer, across nearly four hundred polls.
- The president's approval rating sat between 30 and 39 percent depending on the pollster, with Reuters/Ipsos and the Associated Press recording 31 percent — the lowest of his term and, by one historical comparison, lower than every pre-midterm president since 1950 except George W. Bush in 2006, whose party lost thirty House seats and six Senate seats.
- More than 63 percent of voters told pollsters ICE had "gone too far." Sixty-two percent named the Iran war among the most important issues facing the country — more than immigration or crime.
- A Cygnal survey of nine Senate battleground states found a fourteen-point swing from the 2024 result — a region Trump carried by nine was now leaning Democratic by four, with independents breaking Democratic by thirty points.
- Sixty-three percent of registered voters told Gallup they were worse off than two years earlier — the highest reading since the 2009 recession.

Every one of those numbers has a counter-consideration: midterm polls have missed before (a historic miss in Michigan's Senate primary, documented later, is the standing caution); the House map was redrawn to be wave-resistant; the president's party almost always loses ground, but almost never this much. Beneath the numbers lay a deeper divide, measured by ABC, the Washington Post, and Ipsos in mid-October: six in ten Americans were confident the election would be conducted fairly, but ninety-four percent of Democrats and seventy-seven percent of independents did not believe the president would accept the results if Republicans lost — against sixty-three percent of Republicans who did. Sixty-one percent said he was not committed to free and fair elections. And on the question of expectations, Americans anticipated a Democratic House victory by roughly two to one. The forecasters, whose full record appears in the proper place below, concluded by late October that a Democratic House majority was highly likely and a Democratic Senate majority was more likely than not.

### The Historical Mirror

Three elections anchor every serious attempt to read 2026. In **1918**, Woodrow Wilson — winning the war in Europe, ten days before the vote — publicly demanded a Democratic Congress for peace-leverage reasons; voters, amid the flu's deadliest month and wartime controls, gave Republicans twenty-five House seats and six Senate seats anyway. The lesson analysts draw: war leaders do not get midterm passes. In **1974**, with a president pardoned by his successor eleven months after resigning, amid an oil-shock economy, voters delivered a forty-eight-seat House landslide and a class of ninety-three freshmen who rewrote congressional power for two decades. In **2006**, with a different president at 37 percent approval in a different war, Republicans lost thirty House seats and six Senate seats. In **2002**, the counter-case: a popular war president gained seats. The 2026 environment resembles 2006 in its fundamentals and 1918 in its texture — but differs from every precedent in one respect: no previous wave crashed into a map its own side had redrawn in advance.

### THE HONEST CAVEATS — READ BEFORE VOTING

1. **No candidate is perfectly clean.** Talarico's super PAC took dark money. Peltola's super PAC took pop-up nonprofit money. Conley took leadership-PAC money (the Spotlight PA finding). Jackson's dark-money air cover came from the same anonymous layer he campaigns against. The caveats are on the record, next to the recommendations.

2. **Clean money doesn't guarantee good governance.** A candidate funded by small donors can still vote badly. This list measures money, not judgment. The voting records are in the document's Parts II and III.

3. **The cleanest candidate doesn't always win.** The record shows 53% of 2018's no-PAC-pledge candidates lost. A clean-money vote is a long-game investment in a different system, not a guarantee of this election's outcome.

4. **Third parties and independents are not automatically cleaner.** Bodnar and Osborn are clean; but the spoiler operations (Enough Promises, Lead Left, Real Change) show that "independent" can also be a manufactured label. Check the funder, not the label.

5. **The system is the problem, not just the candidates.** The FEC has no quorum. Dark money is legal. The revolving door is legal. Voting for clean candidates is necessary but not sufficient — the structural fixes (disclosure, public financing, contribution limits) are the ballot measures above.

## PART III — THE SENATE



*What this part covers: the seventeen races that decide control of the upper chamber — who the candidates are, where their money comes from, what the polls say, and what the money bought. If you read only one part, read this one; the Senate is where the election will be won or lost on election night.*

Thirty-five Senate seats are contested: the 33 regularly scheduled, plus special elections in Florida (the remainder of Marco Rubio's term) and Ohio (the remainder of J.D. Vance's term). Republicans defend 22 seats; Democrats defend 13. Democrats need a net gain of four to reach 51; Republicans need to lose no more than two to keep at least 50, with the vice president breaking a tie.

By late October, forecasters rated seven races as genuine toss-ups — six Republican-held and one Democratic-held — with the path to a Democratic majority running through them. This section covers every competitive race in full: the candidates, their money, their records, the polls, and what the money bought. Non-competitive races are listed at the end.

### Texas — The Cycle's Most Expensive Race

Open seat (John Cornyn retired after losing his primary). Trump carried Texas by 13.6 points in 2024.

**James Talarico (D)** is a 37-year-old five-term state representative from Austin — a former sixth-grade teacher in San Antonio's poorest neighborhoods, a seminarian, and the son of a single mother who fled an abusive relationship when he was an infant. He swore off corporate PAC money. His campaign's final accounting showed about $128.8 million raised — 95 percent of it from individuals, with 55 percent of all receipts coming in increments too small to require disclosure, and a median donation of fifty dollars from more than half a million donors. His PAC list consisted entirely of labor unions. He holds the all-time record for a single fundraising quarter in Senate history, roughly $60 million in the third quarter of 2026.

**Ken Paxton (R)** is the sitting attorney general — in office since 2015, impeached by the Texas House in 2023 and acquitted by the Senate, indicted on securities-fraud charges in 2015 and resolved by a 2024 pretrial deal requiring no admission of guilt, and the subject of a $6.6 million whistleblower judgment against his office. He defeated Cornyn in a May runoff, 64 to 36, the first time Texas Republicans had ever denied renomination to one of their own senators. He raised about $35.6 million.

The asymmetry that defines the race is not between the two campaigns but between Talarico's campaign and Paxton's outside support: Elon Musk poured roughly $158 million into the Senate Leadership Fund in September, and the Fund's Texas affiliate — a committee formed in late August — spent more than $148 million against Talarico, at one point booking $61.4 million in two days. A Senate campaign's combined spending in Texas across the same period was outmatched by Musk's spending alone by roughly fifty-six cents on the dollar. In total, more than a quarter of a billion dollars flowed into the Texas Senate race — the largest sum ever spent on a single Senate contest.

The Washington Post reported in October that Paxton, as attorney general, had repeatedly written legal opinions benefiting large donors — including makers of a slot-machine-like device, after his office issued a June 2026 opinion clarifying what counts as gambling at a state senator's request, and an investor in a concrete facility seeking to avoid permitting limits. The Wall Street Journal reported footage appearing to show Paxton at a Dubai New Year's Eve party with two men then under federal indictment for allegedly embezzling $15 million from a Chicago hospital; one of them, Anosh Ahmed, had given Paxton $190,000, including $25,000 days after his indictment. Paxton also accepted roughly $448,000 from data-center developers while his own financial disclosure showed up to $6.8 million in AI-linked assets — campaigning against an industry in which he held stock. He denied wrongdoing throughout.

The campaign's closing weeks were personal: Paxton's side promoted a slur ("Talafreako") and false claims; Talarico turned the attack into merchandise. Paxton was favored by prediction markets near the end, but polls told a different story — a Fox News survey showed a 49–49 tie on October 22, with Talarico holding a narrow average lead. Neil Bush, the Republican president's brother, announced he had voted for Talarico.

### Ohio — The Rematch and the Bribery Shadow

Special election for the final two years of J.D. Vance's term. Trump carried Ohio by 11.2 points.

**Sherrod Brown (D)** — the three-term senator defeated in 2024 — returned to face **Jon Husted (R)**, the appointed incumbent: former secretary of state, former lieutenant governor, appointed to the seat when Vance became vice president.

Brown ran the same small-dollar playbook that defined his career: an average donation of twenty-seven dollars, 84 percent of his money from individuals, and a refusal of corporate PACs. Husted's campaign raised about $19 million — with 510 corporate PACs contributing, including energy, insurance, pharmaceutical, and accounting interests — while outside groups poured in more: the crypto industry alone spent over $46 million attacking Brown, the single largest outside intervention in any 2026 Senate race.

The FirstEnergy scandal runs beneath Husted's candidacy — a $60 million bribery scheme that bought a $1 billion bailout law. Husted was never charged, and fact-checkers noted that attack ads overstate his role. But the documentary record is substantial. $3.5 million in FirstEnergy-linked money ultimately benefited his campaigns, including a $1 million secret 2017 payment. Husted also received $116,892 from the Wexner family — the retail fortune whose founder's ties to Epstein were a recurring subject of the files released in January — and, in the Epstein-files fight, voted to block an amendment compelling further document release. The convicted lobbyist Neil Clark called him the company's "golden boy" in texts. His own calendars showed repeated meetings with FirstEnergy's CEO, the indicted House speaker, and the utility-regulator chair while the bailout was drafted. And he appeared as a defense witness in the executives' retrial, which began September 28, 2026 — days before early voting opened. The data-center fight also landed here: Husted accepted $69,750 from data-center companies and $637,200 from executives of a trading firm expanding into AI, even as the issue became what a Republican Senate committee's internal memo called an "anchor" around his candidacy.

Polls showed Brown ahead by three to six points. One detail unique in the cycle: both campaigns raised exactly $14.4 million through the second quarter — the only race in the country where the two sides matched to the dollar. The governor's race atop the ticket — Democrat Amy Acton leading Republican Vivek Ramaswamy in most surveys — compounded Republican difficulties.

### Maine — The Logger Against the Institution

Republican-held. Susan Collins seeks a sixth term; she has served since 1997 and holds the second-longest consecutive-vote streak in Senate history.

**Troy Jackson (D)**, a fifth-generation logger from Allagash — a town in a county larger than Rhode Island and Connecticut combined — entered the race only in late July 2026, after the original Democratic nominee withdrew amid allegations. Jackson, a former state senate president, raised $20.4 million in under three months — 93 percent from individuals, with a labor-union PAC list and a surge of small donors. Collins raised slightly more, but the character of the money differed: corporate PACs and, more consequentially, more than $7.4 million routed to her support through five opaque nonprofit organizations, one of which alone spent $11 million on her ads.

In September, ProPublica reported the arrangement at the center of this document's money-for-favor stories. Navatek, a Hawaii defense contractor, had routed $150,000 to a pro-Collins super PAC through a shell organization in 2019. An email from the operative who designed the shell called it "very smart" while noting it was illegal. Within weeks, the Senate had allocated $21.5 million for Navatek projects in Maine; two months later, the contractor's chief executive wrote internally that Collins had committed to $32 million in naval contracts.

Collins vigorously denied wrongdoing, noted the Biden Justice Department found no wrongdoing in 2021, and called the story a political hit job. ProPublica responded that the 2021 inquiry covered only allegations through 2021, while the contractor chief's statements to the FBI were made in 2022 through 2024. The Senate Ethics complaint filed in October sat, like everything else, before a federal election watchdog without a quorum. Separately, a lobbyist at the center of the scheme ran a firm that had paid Collins' husband; that same lobbyist ran another pro-Collins super PAC in 2026.

The polls closed to a dead heat: a Fox News survey showed 49–49; three other October polls gave Jackson a one-to-two-point edge. The ranked-choice mechanics — six declared write-ins on the Senate ballot — meant the count could extend days beyond Election Day.

### Georgia — The Filmmaker and the Trucking Heir

Democratic-held. **Jon Ossoff (D)**, elected in 2021's runoff, raised the second-largest sum of any Senate candidate — about $109 million, with 760,000 contributions in the third quarter alone at a $46 average, and a PAC list consisting entirely of labor and professional organizations. His Republican opponent, **Mike Collins**, is a first-term congressman and trucking-company owner whose voting record includes authoring the Laken Riley Act and a no-exceptions anti-abortion position that, per reporting, the president privately worried would cost the race; Collins also carried a House ethics inquiry into his chief of staff and questions raised by USA Today about white-supremacist ties, which he denied.

Ossoff led by seven to eight points in final polls. Georgia's separate machinery loomed over everything: the new secretary of state had been stripped from the election board, the state bureau of investigation had gained election-investigation authority, and a December 1 runoff loomed if no candidate cleared fifty percent.

### North Carolina — The Prosecutor Against the Lobbyist

Open seat (Thom Tillis retired). **Roy Cooper (D)** — the former four-term attorney general and two-term governor who never lost an election in North Carolina — faced **Michael Whatley (R)**, the former Republican National Committee chair. Cooper raised about $35 million with a small-dollar base; Whatley raised about $13 million and entered October with under $1 million on hand.

Whatley had never held elected office. His career ran through lobbying: an early firm, the Patriot Group, with utility clients including Cinergy and Duke Energy; O'Connor & Hannon, where he personally generated $1.3 million from accounts including ExxonMobil, General Electric, and Lockheed Martin; HBW Resources, the energy firm he served for fifteen years and where he took home $8 million-plus from corporate clients including the American Petroleum Institute; and Capital City Ventures, his own firm, operated while he chaired the state and national parties — an arrangement that placed a sitting party chairman inside a lobbying practice. He had also led the Consumer Energy Alliance, a group the journalism organization Mother Jones described as a fossil-fuel front. His financial disclosure showed stock in ExxonMobil, Chevron, and Shell. Cooper's campaign framed him as the fossil-fuel lobbyist; Whatley campaigned on taxes and the border.

By October, forecasters had moved the race from toss-up to leaning Democratic and then further — Cooper led by eight to fourteen points in final polls, and Republican committees canceled advertising reservations, redirecting the money elsewhere. Private estimates held that the race could ultimately total $650 to $800 million, which would break the national record set in Georgia in 2022.

### Michigan — The Purist Against the Committee Man

Open seat (Gary Peters retired). **Abdul El-Sayed (D)** — an epidemiologist, former Detroit health director, and 2018 gubernatorial candidate — won the August primary by one percentage point over the establishment favorite, Representative Haley Stevens, after a campaign in which roughly $33 million in outside money, a record for any House or Senate primary, was spent against him or for his opponent. He accepts no corporate PAC money; his funding is small-dollar and union. **Mike Rogers (R)**, a former FBI agent and House Intelligence Committee chair who lost this seat in 2024 by 19,000 votes, ran again — backed by approximately $95 million in outside support to El-Sayed's $42 million, the most expensive Senate race in Michigan history, including a $25 million commitment from the largest Republican super PAC.

Rogers' donor list included a hospital-system executive, a hedge-fund billionaire, and PACs tied to the DeVos family, the American Petroleum Institute, and the tobacco industry. El-Sayed's list included a union base and a progressive network — plus an asterisk his opponent wielded: a pro-Israel super PAC's record-shattering primary intervention, and roughly $1.1 million in small donations identified as coming from the network of another pro-Israel committee. "Are we going to let $50 million in lies... steal this election?" El-Sayed asked a rally with Bernie Sanders and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez.

Final polls showed El-Sayed ahead by two points. A caution hung over the reading of Michigan polls: nine separate pollsters had shown El-Sayed leading his primary by roughly fifteen points; he won by about one. It was the largest polling miss of the cycle.

### Iowa — The Paralympian and the Anchors

Open seat (Joni Ernst retired). **Josh Turek (D)**, a state representative and two-time Paralympic gold medalist who flipped a Trump-carried district, faced **Ashley Hinson (R)**, a congresswoman and former television anchor. The money told the structural story: Hinson's $10.2 million campaign was 51.9 percent individual, 21.4 percent corporate PAC, and 26.7 percent party transfers — propped up further by $8.76 million in coordinated party money and $56.7 million in outside spending, including $28.7 million from the Senate Leadership Fund. Turek's $6.3 million was 93.9 percent individual; his outside support, $43 million, was led by a Democratic Senate super PAC. In percentage terms, both men ran small-dollar campaigns; in dollars, Hinson's machine outspent Turek's roughly two to one, with the air war even more lopsided.

Hinson had voted seven times against war-powers resolutions to end the Iran conflict before telling reporters in October she would press the administration on the war. Turek led narrowly in most final polls — the race was a true toss-up.

### Alaska — The Fisherman's Daughter and the Marine

Republican-held. **Dan Sullivan (R)**, the incumbent, faced **Mary Peltola (D)** — the former congresswoman and first Alaska Native in Congress's history, who lost to Nick Begich in 2024 and now sought to return via the Senate.

The race became the second-most expensive of the cycle, at roughly $104 million. Peltola raised $32.6 million — about three times the incumbent's total — with a small-dollar base. Sullivan's support included $877,000 routed through a pro-Israel committee's conduit, the largest such flow tracked in any 2026 race, plus Club for Growth and Koch-network money. Two spoiler operations spent millions trying to bleed each candidate from their flanks; one of the spoiler candidates was an incarcerated man in a New York federal prison who had never been to Alaska, advanced through ballot quirks and kept on the ballot by the courts. Alaska's ranked-choice rounds would begin November 18; the seat might not be decided for weeks.

Peltola led narrowly in final polls. The texture beneath the topline told the same story from both directions. Sullivan's support included $398,811 from the trawling industry — itemized company by company, from Trident Seafoods' $138,020 down through the smallest processors' PACs — while Peltola's campaign was 96 percent small-dollar, launched with $1.5 million in its first twenty-four hours, and drew its donations from out-of-state individuals rather than out-of-state industry. Her side carried its own dark-money asterisk: Sludge exposed a nonprofit created that summer funding her super PAC entirely, donors undisclosed — the pop-up architecture operating on both sides of the race.

### Kansas — The Pastor in the Machine's Path

Republican-held. **Roger Marshall (R)** faced **Mark Holland (D)**, a Methodist pastor and former Kansas City mayor. Holland raised a record $3.5 million for a Kansas Democrat; Marshall had the outside machine — the Senate Leadership Fund and allies poured in millions to defend a seat in a state Trump carried by eleven. Polling was a genuine divergence: surveys showed a coin flip, while fundamentals-based models showed Marshall ahead by seven. Prediction markets split the difference. The Senate Leadership Fund's war plan, revealed by the New York Times, allocated defense money here precisely because the fundamentals could no longer be assumed.

### South Carolina — The Sister's Succession

Special election. Senator Lindsey Graham died on July 11, 2026, of an aortic dissection, days after returning from Kyiv. His sister, Darline Graham Nordone — never elected to anything, a state official who had been raised by her brother — was appointed to the seat, then won the Republican primary. She faced **Dr. Annie Andrews (D)**, a pediatrician who raised $10.8 million, 98.8 percent of it from individuals, in a state Trump carried by eighteen. The national party rescued the appointed incumbent with millions in coordinated and outside money after her campaign raised only about $350,000. The rescuing PAC, Security Is Strength, was Lindsey Graham's own machine — funded in its final months by a $1 million gift from Larry Ellison, the Oracle founder whose giving history runs through Marco Rubio's and Mitt Romney's super PACs, and operated by J. Warren Tompkins, the South Carolina strategist Time called "the most powerful non-elected man in Palmetto State politics," who had managed every Graham race since 2002. The race stayed Likely Republican, but the spread between the candidates' organic money — and the fundraising surge in the state's Democratic primary, where turnout doubled — registered with both parties.

A footnote belongs here in its proper place: the late senator's name surfaced posthumously in the Navatek contractor's statements to the FBI, alleging a cash-for-contract arrangement; he was deceased by the time the reporting appeared, and the allegations were unadjudicated.

### Nebraska — The Mechanic and the Heir

Republican-held special. **Dan Osborn (I)** — the industrial mechanic who led a 77-day strike at a Kellogg's plant and nearly unseated a senator in 2024 — ran again, this time against **Pete Ricketts (R)**, the appointed incumbent and heir to the TD Ameritrade fortune. Ricketts self-funded $5.2 million of his $6.3 million; Osborn's average donation was forty dollars, and he pledged to caucus with neither party. The two independent candidates in Montana and South Dakota made the same pledge together at a joint press conference — three working-class candidates promising to deny both party leaders their vote. Polls showed Ricketts ahead by a single point in a state Trump carried by twenty. The campaign's single debate, at the Nebraska State Fair on September 1, produced the exchange that defined it. Ricketts opened with "My opponent, Democrat Dan, is going to lie to you trying to hide his liberal beliefs" — to which Osborn replied, "You know, we really need to get a third podium up here for Democrat Dan." On the war, after a Nebraska servicemember was killed in Iran, Ricketts said a country that chants death to America cannot be allowed a nuclear weapon; Osborn said the United States had to stop digging the hole and give Congress back its power to declare war. On immigration, Ricketts charged that Osborn wanted to abolish ICE; Osborn answered that the country should never get used to troops in its streets and masked agents. Both men opposed the three-hundred-thousand-ton duty-free beef import — the one issue of consensus in the race.

### Minnesota — Two Seats, One State

Two Senate elections effectively ran concurrently. For the open seat of retiring Tina Smith, **Peggy Flanagan (D)** — the lieutenant governor, who would be the first Native American woman in the Senate — faced **Michele Tafoya (R)**, the former NBC Sunday Night Football sideline reporter, who outraised her in consecutive quarters with national party backing. Flanagan led by three to four points. Separately, Klobuchar's decision to run for governor means that if she wins, her Senate seat goes to an appointee until a November 2027 special election. And Minnesota's governor race matched Klobuchar against Lisa Demuth — whose campaign was kept afloat after losing the party convention by $1.1 million from a Wisconsin shipping billionaire, a fact Klobuchar weaponized on the trail: "She can be that guy's choice. I want to be Minnesota's choice."

### Florida and New Hampshire — The Bookends

**Florida:** **Ashley Moody (R)**, the appointed incumbent, led state representative **Anjie Nixon (D)** by four to eleven points; Nixon had defeated a candidate who raised $16 million in the primary. **New Hampshire:** open seat (Jeanne Shaheen retired). **Chris Pappas (D)**, a four-term congressman from a family restaurant, raised $21.5 million — 88 percent individual, with 95 percent of his third-quarter haul under $100 — against former senator **John E. Sununu (R)**, whose $5.3 million campaign was dwarfed by $30.9 million in outside support, the largest piece from Musk-funded committees. Pappas led by seven.

## PART IV — THE HOUSE

*What this part covers: the fight for the 435-seat lower chamber — the redistricting war that redrew the battlefield, the thirty competitive districts where the majority will be decided, the working-class challengers and the comeback candidates, and the civil wars both parties' maps forced upon themselves.*

### The Arithmetic and the Seawall

The House enters Election Day at 218 Republicans, 214 Democrats, one independent (Kevin Kiley of California, who left the Republican Party in March 2026 and caucuses with it for now), and two vacancies. Forty-six seats are rated competitive; all twenty-nine rating changes made by the Cook Political Report since early October moved toward Democrats. The Sabato Crystal Ball's late-October assessment held that Democrats could lose every toss-up race and still win the majority — an extraordinary position reflecting both the environment and the map.

The map deserves its own paragraph, because 2026 is the first midterm ever contested on maps redrawn mid-decade for partisan advantage at this scale. Texas added five Republican-leaning seats; California's voter-approved map added five Democratic ones; Ohio, Florida, North Carolina, Tennessee, Louisiana, and Alabama added Republican seats; Utah gained a Democratic-leaning Salt Lake seat by court order. The failures matter too: Missouri's map was blocked by the Supreme Court; Virginia's was invalidated by its own high court after voters approved it; Indiana's Republican senate killed its map; South Carolina's legislature adjourned without one. Net effect: analysts estimated a Republican structural gain of about twelve seats — a seawall, built in public, tested for the first time on November 3.

### The Thirty-Front Battle

The competitive House map runs through the border (Texas's 9th, 15th, and 34th — three Democratic-held seats in a state that redrew them all), the suburbs (Virginia's 2nd and 10th, New York's 17th and 19th, New Jersey's 7th), the industrial Midwest (Ohio's 1st and 7th, Michigan's 7th and 10th, Pennsylvania's 7th, 8th, and 10th, Iowa's 1st and 3rd, Wisconsin's 3rd), the Mountain West (Colorado's 3rd and 8th, Montana's 1st, Arizona's 1st and 6th), and the coasts (California's 13th, 22nd, 40th, 41st, 45th, and 48th; Washington's 3rd; Maine's 2nd; Alaska's at-large seat).

A handful of these races carry the thematic weight of the entire election:

**Texas's 15th** — the Rio Grande Valley seat — produced the cycle's cleanest three-way divergence. Incumbent Republican Monica De La Cruz faced Bobby Pulido, a Tejano singer with two Latin Grammys who raised $6.2 million, 93 percent from individuals, with ninety percent of his second-quarter haul under $100. Polling showed Pulido ahead by five to seven points in a district Trump carried by eighteen; prediction markets priced a Pulido win above eighty cents; a panel of forecasting models favored De La Cruz. The incumbent carried her own scandals — a $2.4 million earmark to a nonprofit whose founder was arrested for theft days before their joint appearance, leaked fundraiser audio, and attacks on her residency. One poll found 60 percent of the district's independents backing Pulido.

**Iowa's 1st** — where Christina Bohannan, a law professor whose father lost his insurance while dying of emphysema, ran her third campaign against Mariannette Miller-Meeks, who won the seat by six votes in 2020 and by 799 in 2024. ## PART V — THE MONEY

*What this part covers: the eleven-billion-dollar question beneath every other chapter — who funded this election, through what mechanisms, and with what visible return. This part answers the question a voter is entitled to ask of any candidate: whose money is it?*

### The Totals

More than $15 billion was raised for federal elections in the cycle, and more than $11 billion was spent on advertising alone — exceeding the $11.2 billion spent in the 2024 presidential year. A midterm outspending a presidential cycle had never happened before. The concentration beneath the record: the top thirty House districts absorbed roughly 65 percent of all House super-PAC money — the system's gravity pulling every dollar toward the same few races while the rest of the map went dark. The per-race anatomy, at the final accounting: Ohio past $460 million, Texas past $500 million, Maine at $260 million in its Senate race alone, and Michigan's 7th — a single House district — at $60 million, the most expensive House race in the documented record, with Florida's 6th and Iowa's House battlegrounds near $50 million each.

Outside spending approached $2 billion, with the six largest committees — the Senate and House leadership funds on both sides, the Texas Senate committee, and the Koch network's action arm — accounting for a third of it; sixteen organizations accounted for half.

The state-level mirror: the Democratic legislative committee budgeted $50 million, its largest ever, against a Republican counterpart that raised $49 million in the last comparable cycle. Outside groups on the Democratic side had outspent the Republican constellation $175 million to $49 million in 2024. A veteran Democratic strategist's verdict on the allocation fight inside his own party: "I've watched this party pour $110 million into Jaime Harrison's campaign against Lindsey Graham. That was a fool's errand. How many Michigan Senate seats could we have picked up for that?"

The judicial layer beneath: one network had spent $29 million on state supreme courts since 2014; the year's one flippable high-court majority, in North Carolina, drew $2 million on one side and nothing yet on the other.

The Federal Election Commission's own statistics show the architecture plainly: super PAC and hybrid committee money of roughly $6.7 billion dwarfs the $2.8 billion raised by candidates themselves.

### The Industries

Three industries bought their way into the first rank of campaign finance in 2026, alongside the traditional sectors:

**Cryptocurrency.** Roughly $206 million made crypto the largest corporate contributor of the cycle — more than double its 2024 total. The Fairshake network entered the year with $193 million on hand and won fifty-three of fifty-seven primaries it contested. Its money bought one defining vote: on September 15, the Senate's most consequential crypto bill, the CLARITY Act, failed to advance 49–50, with every Democrat and four Republicans opposed — a bill the industry's lobbyists had built toward all year, which may return in the lame-duck session.

**Artificial intelligence.** Meta committed $65 million to state-level super PACs fighting AI regulation; a rival firm pledged $20 million for pro-regulation candidates — the industry purchasing both sides of its own rulebook. A Trump-aligned committee staffed by former campaign officials planned $100 million on a lighter-regulation agenda; another, founded by OpenAI's president, spent on federal preemption of state rules. Both parties' candidates took AI money while campaigning on data-center moratoria — the industry's hedge at full scale.

Corporate federal political spending of $646 million through the second quarter of 2026 set an all-time midterm high — forty percent above all of 2024 — even before the final surge.

**Sports betting.** Some $72 million through a single trade-organization vehicle made gambling the third-largest industry donor, funding primaries on both sides against its regulators — including nearly $1 million against an Ohio state representative who had filed mobile-betting-ban legislation.

The traditional sectors kept their lanes. Oil and gas: Chevron, Occidental, and the American Petroleum Institute gave millions to the Senate Leadership Fund, and the API's former client Michael Whatley ran for Senate in North Carolina. Pharmaceuticals: a career $1 million-plus to Michigan's Mike Rogers, who voted repeatedly against Medicare drug-price negotiation. Insurance and utilities. Defense. Tobacco. And organized labor — the one conduit that out-raised its corporate counterpart at the traditional PAC level, with labor's committee receipts exceeding corporate PACs' in the FEC's own accounting, and 98.8 percent of the four largest unions' direct candidate money going to Democrats. Dues-funded union politics and corporate-funded dark money are recorded here side by side, as the two conduit systems they are.

### The Access Economy

Beyond campaigns, the second term produced a new category of political money with no election attached. The first is a presidential ballroom fund, which collected $333 million from sixty-seven large donors. The second is a presidential library fund, which collected as much as $63 million in settlement payments from media companies into an entity that Florida administratively dissolved — the money's whereabouts unknown — while a second, differently named entity reported $50 million received. No law requires disclosure of any of it.

The ballroom ledger, tier by tier, from the fund's own records: $25 million each from Apple, Nvidia, the Adelson Family Foundation, and EchoStar's Charles Ergen. $22 million from Google, routed through a lawsuit settlement. $10 million each from Lockheed Martin, Microsoft, Palantir, and a mortgage-lending subsidiary — the last arriving weeks after federal approval of its parent company's acquisition. $5 million each from Amazon (with a federal antitrust trial pending), T-Mobile (with spectrum matters before regulators), Meta, Cisco, and ServiceNow. At least $77 million — twenty-three percent of the total — came from donors the fund agreed to keep anonymous. The president, despite describing himself as a donor, does not appear on the list. The Venezuelan banking family's pledge of $2.5 million sits inside the anonymous tier, with $835,000 paid on October 14, 2025; the family's patriarch received his full pardon on January 15, 2026, five days before the consulting contract with the former personal attorney took effect. A "Gold Card" program sold expedited residency for $1 million per individual and $2 million per corporate employee, with a $5 million tier pending in Congress. The access economy's remaining inventory, itemized in the research files, runs to ten items. A $400 million Qatari aircraft. Up to $10 million in fees from a short-video platform's restructured ownership. A golden share in a steelmaker, held personally by the president and expiring at term's end. $63 million in media-settlement payments to the dissolved library entity. A $15 million settlement from one network, structured as a charitable contribution. $23 billion in emergency arms to Gulf states. A $620 million Pentagon loan to a metals company three months after a presidential son took a stake. $13 million in no-bid contracts to a January 6 event firm. And a cabinet whose members' flows to the president's interests exceeded $30 million by one accounting. Each entry carries a denial, a defense, or a procedural explanation in its proper place; the inventory itself is the record. A federal court found, in October, that the administration had diverted border-security appropriations to finance a national advertising campaign — "The Country He Loves" — after a strategy meeting in which, according to the plaintiffs' account, advisers identified specific candidates to help. The judge found the challengers likely to succeed. And a New York Times investigation traced a $50 million consulting contract to the president's former personal attorney — awarded while she simultaneously served as the interim U.S. attorney — to a Venezuelan banking family whose patriarch received a full pardon weeks later, after his daughter had donated $3.5 million to the president's super PAC and pledged $2.5 million to the ballroom. All parties denied wrongdoing; a campaign-finance complaint alleging the daughter acted as a straw donor was filed into an FEC without a quorum.

### The Middlemen and the Predatory Edges

Of the $11 billion spent on ads, the largest share passed through five media-buying firms that collected $2.1 billion in the prior cycle, typically on commissions near fifteen percent. One Democratic firm took $858 million, ninety percent of it from three clients; one Republican firm took $336 million, ninety-eight percent from a single client — the Trump family's super PAC. Below them, the consulting tier: the largest Republican firm reported $196 million in net revenue. An academic study of the trade found Republican-side firms charge higher markups and exert less effort than their Democratic counterparts.

Below the campaigns, an FBI warning in October identified scam PACs siphoning donations from elderly donors, AI-voiced donation fraud, and merchandise schemes; the conviction record for such operations stretches back a decade, and one legal scheme — pre-checked recurring donation boxes — produced refund requests from 12 percent of the Trump campaign's online donors in 2020, emptying some low-income donors' accounts.

And a 2022 Supreme Court decision eliminating limits on candidate loan repayments produced a quiet secondary market. Candidates lent their campaigns $203 million, then retired the debts with post-election donations — money that, in the scholars' framing, flows back into the candidate's own pocket. The named record: in Ohio, a senator who lent his campaign $4.5 million — bank-financed at seven percent — had $1.25 million repaid on January 20, 2026, from post-election donations. In Texas, a border-district representative loaned $1.9 million, had $500,000 repaid, and reinstated the remaining $1.4 million on August 31, 2026, to run repayment through November 3. In Nevada, a candidate lent himself $1.6 million across two cycles. In North Carolina, a Senate candidate repaid $790,000 of a $1 million House loan, then $539,000 of a $765,000 Senate loan after withdrawing. In Oklahoma, a cabinet-bound senator repaid his $1 million special-election loan in full by December 2025. In California, a former member carried $787,000 in outstanding loans he later revoked as forgiven.

### The Foreign Layer

The legal foundation: a federal statute, 52 U.S.C. §30121, bars foreign nationals from making contributions, donations, or independent expenditures in American elections — and bars anyone from soliciting or accepting them. Enforcement runs through two doors: criminal prosecution by the Justice Department and civil action by the Federal Election Commission.

The criminal door produced one sentencing this cycle — a straw-donor operator who had funneled roughly 1,200 illegal contributions — and one live complaint against a sitting member of Congress alleging that a Haiti-tied company had channeled money to her campaign; she resigned her Florida seat days after the complaint was filed. The municipal template for the mechanics had been set the year before in New York, where a Turkish-straw-donor scheme — benefits worth more than $100,000 in exchange for access to $10 million in public matching funds — ended with the charges dismissed at the Justice Department's request, a dismissal the new mayor revoked in January 2026.

The civil door led into the void. The Herrera straw-donor complaint — alleging that the Venezuelan banker's daughter had served as a conduit for her father's $3.5 million in super PAC donations — was filed with an election commission that has lacked a quorum since May 2025 and has sat on a two-hundred-case backlog while roughly a quarter of all political spending went undisclosed. An April 2025 presidential memo directed the Justice Department to probe the Democratic fundraising platform for straw and foreign-nexus contributions; a congressional probe cited twenty-two "significant fraud campaigns," nearly half with a foreign nexus, and 237 donations from foreign internet addresses in a single 2024 window. The platform's defenders noted the investigation's timing and the absence of any demonstrated outcome-level fraud.

At the state level, a different door: twenty-five states now ban foreign money from ballot-measure campaigns, eighteen of them covering indirect routes through nonprofits and limited-liability companies, after Ohio's ban survived constitutional challenge in November 2025. In Congress, legislation introduced in July 2026 by Senators Raskin and Whitehouse targets the post-Citizens-United loophole for foreign-owned corporations — foreign ownership of American stocks having risen from four to forty percent between 1986 and 2020 — so that a corporation substantially owned abroad could no longer spend in American elections as a domestic entity.

And the historical shelf holds the template: the 2019 case of Lev Parnas and Igor Fruman, two Soviet-born businessmen who routed $325,000 from the Russian businessman Andrew Muraviev through a shell energy company, Global Energy Producers, into an American campaign committee — and whose text messages documented the access economy in real time. The same architecture, on the record, that the Herrera complaint alleges in miniature.

### The Vacuum

Every thread in this part runs through one enabling condition: the referees are absent. The FEC lacks a quorum. The Election Assistance Commission lost its entire commission in July 2026. A federal judge found the Justice Department's voter-roll demands unlawful; twenty-six judges had dismissed similar demands. An immigration-enforcement agency ran door-to-door voter-status interviews until a federal court halted them days before the election. Through it all, campaigns on both sides filed complaints knowing no quorum existed to hear them — "everyone knows you can do whatever you want this time around," as one watchdog put it. This is not a claim about which side benefits; it is the documented condition under which all of the above occurred.

The plumbing, in figures. Four nonprofits that never disclose a donor serve as the funding wells for the four congressional super PACs: One Nation passed $138 million to the Senate Leadership Fund; the American Action Network passed $64 million to the House Republican committee; House Majority Forward passed $95 million to the Democratic House committee; Majority Forward passed $104 million to the Democratic Senate committee. The four super PACs together raised $714.6 million, and their dark-money affiliates supplied more than one of every four dollars.

The National Republican Senatorial Committee prevailed at the Supreme Court in June, freeing it to coordinate unlimited spending with candidates; the Democratic committee has made almost no use of the same freedom.

The Federal Election Commission's own eighteen-month statistics, published October 1, record the architecture beneath it all. Candidates raised $2.80 billion and spent $2.04 billion, carrying debts of $366.7 million. Party committees raised $1.38 billion. PACs raised $8.31 billion — including $2.49 billion by independent-expenditure-only committees, $4.23 billion by hybrid committees connected to nonprofits, and $305.7 million by leadership PACs, the members' own secondary accounts. Corporate PACs gave $311.4 million; labor PACs $328.8 million; trade associations $136.6 million. Independent expenditures reported through June 30 totaled $643.4 million — a figure the autumn surge multiplied several times over.

## PART VII — THE MACHINERY

*What this part covers: how the vote is actually conducted and counted — early voting, the new laws, the courts, the observers, the ballot measures, and the comparative record of how other democracies run the same machinery.*

### Early Voting

More than twenty million Americans voted before Election Day — 20.6 million at the final snapshot, against roughly 16 million at the comparable point in the last midterm, with eleven days of voting still to go in most states. Registered Democrats had cast about 49 percent of early ballots, Republicans 34 percent, others 17 percent. The state ledgers: Texas up 74 percent on its 2022 pace, with one Harris County Monday recording the largest midterm single day in its history; Kansas more than doubled; Ohio's in-person early vote quadrupled its prior midterm level; North Carolina's Democrats cast more than twice the Republican early ballots; Georgia passed 1.3 million; North Carolina passed 1.1 million; Iowa stood at 167,000. Black and Latino early participation ran three to four times prior levels in Georgia and North Carolina, while white participation in North Carolina ran below its 2018 and 2022 pace. The standing caution, from the University of Florida's Michael McDonald: Election Day tends to be a Republican day, and early-vote leads have misled before. The partisan composition of early voting is a clue, not a result: Election Day itself traditionally skews Republican, and the analyst Michael McDonald's standing caution — the early numbers can create a "mirage" in either direction — applies to every projection built on them. Two structural findings mattered more than the daily totals. A federal court's examination of a postal-service network redesign found that areas subject to the change had seen late-ballot rejections roughly double in the primaries, with about 40 percent of all mail ballots originating in affected areas. And North Carolina — which adopted stricter mail-ballot rules, transferred election-board appointment power to the state auditor, and left a multi-state election-data partnership — saw its absentee requests run 32 percent below 2022 and rejection rates in its largest counties reach 7 to 8 percent, roughly double the prior rate, in a 27-county monitor's sample. The national rejection baseline: roughly 584,000 mail ballots were rejected in 2024 — 1.2 percent of those returned — with signature problems accounting for 40.7 percent; more than half of the ballots that entered the cure process were ultimately counted. The fifty-state cure landscape runs from five business days after the election in California to election-night-only in Iowa, Montana, Wisconsin, and a handful of others, with Pennsylvania and Missouri offering no statewide cure at all.

### The Law

The Supreme Court decided the cycle's defining election case in June: *Watson v. Republican National Committee* held, 5–4, that federal law does not require ballots to arrive by Election Day — states may count mail ballots postmarked by Election Day and received after. Roughly thirty states have grace periods; the ruling protected them. The same term, the Court struck down limits on party-committee coordination with candidates, freeing national parties to function, in Justice Kagan's dissenting phrase, as the candidate's "checking account" — a decision the Republican committees immediately exploited and the Democratic committees largely did not, producing an asymmetric flood of coordinated money in the Senate races.

Below the Court, a war of attrition. The administration's executive order restricting mail voting was blocked by three federal courts and finally abandoned for 2026 when the Supreme Court declined to stay the injunctions. The Justice Department's demands for full statewide voter rolls were dismissed by twenty-six federal judges. A federal judge's preliminary injunction against the Department of Homeland Security's door-to-door voter-status interviews in nine states took effect October 23 — the administration let the appeal window close without filing — barring the sweeps through Election Day while expressly preserving investigations of actual unlawful voting. The nineteen-page ruling found the target lists "voluminous and unreliable," documented the twelve-minute-per-name clearance standard, and grounded the order in the Voting Rights Act's prohibition on intimidating voters and those who assist them. And and the Justice Department sued California to force officials to set aside some twenty thousand ballots based on a list that state officials said contained names of U.S.-born citizens. The case was transferred to U.S. District Judge David O. Carter — the Clinton appointee who had dismissed the department's earlier voter-roll suit, ruling that "there cannot be unbridled consolidation of all elections power in the Executive" — after the drive containing the list arrived with its password on a Post-it note and the state's sample review of two hundred names found most were citizens who had never voted. California's attorney general called the suit "a reckless political stunt"; the twenty-eight prosecutions announced the same week amounted to 0.0001 percent of the state's twenty-three million registered voters. Kansas's law requiring ballots to arrive by poll-close was blocked by a state court.
What changed on the ground, in sum: new photo-ID rules — North Carolina extended its ID requirement to mail ballots for the first time under a 2026 law that also moved board appointment power to the state auditor, Arizona adopted a universal voter-ID requirement by ballot measure in August, and Ohio and California voted on constitutional ID measures in November — counting-time changes, the postal redesign's documented effect — and no validated instance of outcome-determinative fraud presented by any party.

### The Observers

For the count itself: the Justice Department notified counties in at least five states of its plans to deploy roughly a thousand monitors — the largest such effort in the department's history, according to its own leadership; Senate Democrats fielded a hundred trained staff across nine states; the House's bipartisan observer program had hundreds of staff volunteers. The international monitors who have observed every U.S. election since 2002 were not invited — a first. The comparative record deserves one paragraph. Israel's election commission — paper ballots, hand-signed protocols, an offline central count, and public web posting of the handwritten precinct records — is, in its own official's phrase, structured so that anyone who believes it can be rigged might as well believe the Earth is flat. The United Kingdom ran a pilot in May 2026 that processed three million posts, identified two hundred nineteen political deepfakes from sixty accounts reaching 9.4 million views, and then discovered a regulatory grey zone when a platform refused a polling-day removal. The American system's own 2000 failure — networks calling Florida for the wrong candidate twice in one night, against competitive pressure that one anchor later described, on the record, as including corporate owners urging calls that served their business and political interests — remains the canonical anatomy of how calls fail. In 2026, every major network except the public broadcaster draws its data from the Associated Press while making its own calls; one network applies a 99.5 percent confidence standard. One old tool is gone for good. The bellwether county — Vigo County, Indiana, which picked the presidential winner sixteen straight times from 1956 through 2016; the nineteen counties that held perfect streaks since 1980 — died in 2020 and 2024, when every one of them missed. Zero perfect bellwethers remain, and election-night analysts have replaced county magic with district-level signals: Virginia's 2nd as the first wave-detector, the suburban Pennsylvania counties, the Texas border. A federal court ordered ballot-processing protections in Pennsylvania counties, where mail ballots cannot be touched before 7 a.m. on Election Day; Michigan counts late into the night; California accepts mail for seven more days. Alaska and Maine run ranked-choice rounds in the weeks after the vote; Georgia holds a December 1 runoff if needed. The calls on election night — which networks make independently against a 99.5 percent confidence standard at one major outlet — will be provisional, and the certified results, the only ones that count, arrive on the calendars this document carries in the research files.

## PART VIII — ELECTION NIGHT AND BEYOND

*What this part covers: what happens after the polls close — when results arrive, what can go wrong, who governs the lame-duck months, and the leadership and succession fights the election will set in motion.*
